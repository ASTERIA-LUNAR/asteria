Solar power & battery calculator

First-order estimate

First-order estimate: no temperature effects, dust, degradation, wiring/conversion losses or light reflected from terrain. Power = 1361 W/m² (nominal total solar irradiance, IAU 2015 Resolution B3) × (1 AU / Sun distance)² × area × efficiency × cos(incidence) × visible fraction of the solar disk.

Example values - enter your mission's values. The panel area, efficiency, load and usable battery fraction below are placeholders, not data for any real lander.

At the pole the Sun stays near the horizon, so a flat (horizontal) panel sees it almost edge-on, while a vertical panel turned toward the Sun faces it nearly head-on (power ∝ cos(incidence)). The battery must carry the load through the longest darkness; only part of a battery's capacity is usable, so the needed capacity is load × hours of darkness ÷ usable fraction.

About ASTERIA

ASTERIA (Lunar South Pole Landing Site Navigator) is an entry for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 challenge "Lunar Landing Site Navigator". It shows, for sites at the lunar south pole and any date, when the Sun is above the local terrain horizon (solar power and heat) and when the Earth is above it (direct-to-Earth radio contact). At the pole the Sun skims the horizon, so a few metres of hill decide between light and darkness.

Not affiliated with NASA's ASTERIA CubeSat mission. Not endorsed by NASA. This is an independent hackathon project. It is not a mission planning product and must not be used for operational decisions.

Workflow: pick a site on the map → set the mission requirements (Compare page) → compare sites and dates → open the Mission casefile → verify each number (each casefile item links to its method on the Validation page, and "Verify with NASA" opens the JPL Horizons query for that site and time).

What is computed and what is estimated

  • Computed: Sun and Earth positions (validated against JPL Horizons), terrain horizons from LOLA elevation data, rise/set events, visibility windows and their statistics, the launch-slip heatmap, the illumination cross-check.
  • Estimate (labelled in the app): the power and battery calculator (first-order), Earth-link usability (geometric line-of-sight only).
  • Approximations (labelled): terrain from coarser 240 m data for points you click or type inside the map (less precise than the curated sites; validated, see the Validation tab); a smooth-Moon horizon (no mountains) for points outside the map and the reference sites outside the south pole; single landform-centre points for two Artemis III regions whose boundaries are not plotted; the hillshade map uses synthetic lighting.

Methods

  • Ephemeris: Sun, Earth and Moon positions from Astronomy Engine (VSOP87 and a Chapront-based lunar theory). Moon orientation from the IAU/WGCCRE rotation model. Positions are corrected for light travel time and stellar aberration. Azimuth is measured clockwise from local north (toward the lunar north pole). No refraction (the Moon has no atmosphere). Libration is included automatically.
  • Validation: compared against NASA/JPL Horizons on the angle cases and the event times listed on the Validation tab, plus a blind test on freshly fetched cases.
  • Terrain horizons (curated south-pole sites): LOLA gridded topography (LDEM_85S_20M 20 m/px, LDEM_80S_80M 80 m/px, LDEM_75S_120M 120 m/px), ray-marched in 360 directions out to 300 km, observer 2 m above the ground, lunar curvature included. Points clicked or typed inside the map: the same method in the browser on a 240 m version of the same data.
  • Events (sunrise/sunset, Earthrise/Earthset): the centre of the Sun or Earth crossing the horizon (plus the minimum link elevation for Earth), found by a 10-minute scan (60 min for windows over 30 days) and refined by bisection to 1 minute.
  • Windows and statistics: from the sampled series; windows cut by the start or end of the period count only their part inside it.
  • Animation: the Sky view and timeline step through computed samples only (every 10 or 60 minutes); nothing is interpolated between samples.
  • Launch slip: one series covers the whole slip range; each landing date is an exact slice of it, so slip 0 matches the Explore timeline for the same window.
  • Power (first-order estimate): 1361 W/m² (IAU 2015 Resolution B3) × (1 AU / Sun distance)² × area × efficiency × cos(incidence) × visible solar-disk fraction. Battery = load × longest darkness ÷ usable fraction.
  • Map: polar stereographic tiles made locally from the LOLA DEMs (hillshade with synthetic lighting; slope from the DEM) and from NASA's published average-illumination maps (PGDA product 69: 60 m map for about 85°S-90°S, 120 m map beyond). Same projection as the DEMs, so markers and tiles line up. The map only shows the area covered by the elevation data.

Design decisions

Each important engineering choice in one short paragraph: what was chosen and why (plain language first), then the alternatives and how it was checked. Also in DESIGN_DECISIONS.md.

1. Astronomy Engine with the IAU Moon orientation, not SPICE

Plain language: the positions of the Sun and Earth in the lunar sky come from a small open-source library that runs in any browser, so ASTERIA works offline from a single folder.

Technical: Astronomy Engine 2.1.19 (VSOP87 planets, Chapront-based Moon), the IAU/WGCCRE Moon rotation model, light-time correction and first-order stellar aberration, no refraction. SPICE (NAIF kernels: DE440 + the lunar PA frame) is more precise (it uses the Moon's physical librations from the ephemeris) but needs tens of megabytes of kernels and a native or WebAssembly toolkit. Validation against JPL Horizons (which uses those kernels): 52 angle comparisons, elevation within 0.0038° (azimuth 0.055° only with the Sun at 89° elevation); one year hourly at a custom point, elevation within 0.0028°; 10 blind cases within 0.0029°; 336/336 independent status decisions agree. These errors are hundreds of times smaller than the terrain effects ASTERIA shows.

2. Observer 2 m above the ground

Plain language: the horizon is computed for an eye or camera about as high as a lander's instruments, not for the bare ground, where a pebble would block the Sun.

Technical: 2 m is the observer height of NASA's PGDA average-illumination maps (product 69), which ASTERIA is checked against; 10 m was also computed for comparison (VALIDATION_REPORT.md, illumination cross-check). The 3D Walk view and the "Site eye level" preset use the same 2 m.

3. Sun up = the Sun's centre above the terrain horizon; power uses the visible disk fraction

Plain language: "Sun up" means the middle of the Sun is above the skyline. Solar power counts how much of the Sun's disk is actually visible, because a half-hidden Sun gives about half the power.

Technical: a centre crossing gives exact event times (1-minute refinement) and the same rule for the Sun and Earth. The disk fraction (Sun diameter about 0.53°) is used for power and for the comparison with NASA's maps, which average the visible disk fraction. The cross-check reports all three definitions (average disk fraction, centre up, any part up), so the difference is visible rather than hidden.

4. Terrain horizon: 1° azimuth mask, rays to 300 km

Plain language: for each site the skyline is measured in 360 directions, out to 300 km - far enough to include every mountain that can rise above the horizon at the pole.

Technical: tools/terrain.py ray-marches the LOLA DEMs with exact lunar curvature (spherical Moon, R = 1737.4 km), observer 2 m above the bilinear surface; the browser interpolates the mask linearly between whole degrees. Checked: the stored 1° mask vs the horizon computed at the exact azimuth of the Sun or Earth differs by at most 0.050° (mean 0.005°) in the instant-status check. Beyond 300 km a peak would have to be kilometres high to rise above the curvature drop (about 26 km at 300 km).

5. Which LOLA elevation data, at which resolution

Plain language: ASTERIA uses NASA's laser-altimeter maps of the Moon's south pole: the finest map that covers each place.

Technical: LDEM_85S_20M (20 m, 85°S to the pole) near the sites, LDEM_80S_80M (80 m) and LDEM_75S_120M (120 m) farther out and for sites near 80°S, LDEM_875S_5M (5 m, preliminary) for the 2 km patches of three sites in 3D. One product per grid (no mixing inside a grid, so no hidden seams). Every stored 3D grid point (14.5 million since round 6) is within 0.10 m of a direct sample of its source DEM (independent Python check).

6. 240 m data for clicked points, behind a validation gate

Plain language: you can click any point on the map and get a terrain horizon within seconds; it uses a coarser elevation grid so the browser can do it, and this was only switched on after it passed a test.

Technical: tools/coarse_dem.py makes a 240 m area-mean grid (8.8 MB, loaded on the first click); the Web Worker reproduces terrain.py on that grid to 1e-9°. The gate (tests/coarse_validation.js): curated sites differ from their 20 m horizons by 2.0 percentage points of sunlit time on average (6.9 at most; limits 5 / 10), and 50 random points in 85-90°S differ from NASA's 60 m map by 1.5 points on average (limit 5). Alternative considered: smooth-Moon horizons for clicked points (much worse at the pole) or shipping the 20 m DEM (hundreds of MB).

7. Sampling every 10 or 60 minutes, events refined to 1 minute

Plain language: the app checks the sky every 10 minutes (every hour for long periods) and then pins each sunrise, sunset, Earthrise and Earthset down to the minute.

Technical: a 10-minute scan (60 minutes for windows longer than 30 days) followed by bisection to 1 minute. At the pole the Sun's centre moves about 0.003° per minute, so a minute is about the size of the ephemeris error. Event times vs Horizons: within 0.76 min (4 events) and 1.42 min (24 events over a year, including the angle error). Windows shorter than one sampling step can be missed; this is stated in the app. Statistics come from the samples and are accurate to about one sampling step.

8. Tolerances used in the tests

Plain language: every test compares ASTERIA with an independent source and passes only within a stated limit.

Technical: angles vs Horizons: elevation within 0.1° and azimuth within 0.2° (relaxed as 0.2°/cos(el) above 80° elevation; observed elevation ≤ 0.0038°); event times within 2 minutes; Earth orientation within 1° (observed 0.155°); 3D heights within 1 m of the DEM (observed 0.10 m, the 0.1-0.2 m storage step); rover slope within the bound the storage step allows (0.20° at 20 m, 0.81° at 5 m); clicked-point terrain: 5 / 10 percentage points (curated sites), 5 points (random points vs NASA's map); status decisions: 100 % agreement outside a 0.05° "on the edge" band; 3D shading consistency must not decrease from one version to the next.

9. Power model assumptions

Plain language: the power calculator is a first-order estimate: sunlight strength, panel size, efficiency, panel angle and how much of the Sun is visible. It ignores dust, heat and wiring losses.

Technical: P = 1361 W/m² (IAU 2015 Resolution B3 nominal total solar irradiance) × (1 AU / d)² × area × efficiency × cos(incidence) × visible disk fraction; energy by the trapezoid rule on the samples; battery = load × longest darkness ÷ usable fraction. Not modelled: temperature, dust, degradation, conversion losses, light reflected from terrain, panel self-shadowing and height, battery efficiency (all listed in the app). A thermal/electrical model would need hardware-specific data a planner would bring.

10. 3D view: levels of detail and normal maps (round 6)

Plain language: the 3D view draws fine terrain near the site and coarser terrain farther away so it stays fast; where the far terrain is drawn coarsely, its shading still uses the finest real elevation data available there.

Technical: nested square meshes (5 m patch, 20 m tile, ring A to 16 km, ring B to 40 km) sharing their edge vertices; normal maps baked in the browser from the finest loaded LOLA grid (25 m from LDEM_85S_20M where it covers ring A; 100 m for ring B) - never finer than the grid; the amount of detail fades toward a coarser neighbour (mipmaps of real normals) instead of stepping. Geometry morphing between levels was rejected because it would move drawn vertices away from their DEM heights. A camera-centred second shadow map (same Sun direction and casters) sharpens near shadows; the picture uses the minimum of both maps, so it never lights ground the full map shadows. Validated by the height check, seam test and shading consistency (Low 97.8 %, Medium 99.0 %, High 99.8 %, unchanged).

11. The rover is illustrative

Plain language: the 3D rover has the size and shape of NASA's VIPER so you can judge scale, and it sits on the real terrain, but it does not simulate driving: ASTERIA does not predict whether a rover can cross a slope.

Technical: VIPER's published dimensions (2.5 m tall, 1.5 m × 1.5 m, four wheel modules; NASA); other sizes approximate and labelled. Each wheel rests on the LOLA surface and the body's pitch and roll follow from the four wheel heights (at the DEM's 5-20 m resolution, so the tilt is the DEM slope over about one cell). No traction, slip, power, wheel-soil or obstacle physics; the slope warning is only a user-set threshold on the DEM slope.

12. Earthshine as an optional, physically scaled light (round 6)

Plain language: in shadow, the only natural light near the pole is sunlight reflected by Earth. ASTERIA can add it with its real direction and strength, and shows it with a raised exposure like a long camera exposure.

Technical: strength = Earth's geometric albedo 0.434 (NASA Earth Fact Sheet) × (Earth radius / distance)² × the Lambert-sphere phase law at the computed phase angle (about 1/8,400 of sunlight at full Earth); off when Earth's centre is below the validated terrain horizon. Approximate to about a factor of 2 (clouds, Earth's real phase curve). Light reflected from sunlit terrain (which can be brighter) is not modelled. Display only.

13. 3D display defaults: shadows off, relief by mode, stars on (round 6B)

Plain language: when the 3D view opens, the terrain is lit from the Sun's real direction without cast shadows, and stars are shown. Whether the site is in sunlight always comes from the validated horizon model in the status tiles, not from the picture, so the approximate shadows are an option rather than the default.

Technical: cast shadows are a shadow-mapping approximation limited to the drawn terrain (16-40 km), while the status uses rays to 300 km; with shadows on, the picture agrees with the validated status in 97.8 / 99.0 / 99.8 % of 400 cases per quality level (unchanged). Shadows off also skips the shadow passes. Relief shading is off in Orbit and on in Walk/Rover (kept per mode); its contrast is scaled by the layer's grid spacing divided by the ground size of a pixel (at most 1), so relief smaller than about half a pixel fades out using averages of the real DEM normals - this removed the visible 6 km square (luminance difference at the tile edge +49 % -> +1 % on High). Stars are background points with no effect on any calculation, readout, hit test, shadow or status (tested). The About page shows the same entry.

14. Ground-level shading without invented detail (round 6C)

Plain language: standing on the ground in Walk or Rover, the terrain looks smoother than from orbit because the finest elevation data are 5 m and 20 m grids, and one grid cell then fills tens to thousands of screen pixels. ASTERIA shades the real shapes as clearly as it can and does not add any detail to the data. (Amended in round 6E: an illustrative close-range surface texture is now drawn in Walk and Rover only, as a display layer over the unchanged data; see decision 15.)

Technical: the 6 km tile and the 5 m patch are shaded per pixel from normal maps of their own LOLA grids (like the rings since round 6), with anisotropic texture filtering at the device maximum. Relief shading is now a bounded factor (0.4-1.6) on the Lommel-Seeliger brightness that follows the ratio of sunlight on the DEM normal to sunlight on a smoothed DEM normal; its earlier additive form clipped sunlit slopes to black at a low Sun (Mons Malapert in Rover: mean luminance 7.9 of 255). Optional openness shading (sky-view factor from the LOLA heights, Zakšek et al. 2011) is display only and off by default because it did not increase the measured detail. A lunar-Lambert or Hapke photometric model was considered and not used: the Lambert limit gave less ground-level contrast, and the opposition and roughness terms describe effects below the grid. Heights, slopes, readouts, status tiles, Sun/Earth directions and the shading-consistency score (97.8 / 99.0 / 99.8 %) are unchanged (tested).

15. Close-range surface texture in Walk and Rover (round 6E, amended in round 6F; Illustrative)

Plain language: Standing or driving on the ground, the terrain now shows fine grain, small undulations, small craters and a few rock flecks near the viewer, like a carpet laid over the real floor. This texture is illustrative: it is drawn for realism and is not measured. The shape of the ground underneath, and every number ASTERIA shows, still come only from the LOLA data and the validated engine.

Technical: Why: the owner decided that Walk and Rover should look like standing on a real lunar surface; with 5 m and 20 m grids one cell fills tens to thousands of pixels (decision 14). What it is: a procedural pattern computed in the terrain fragment shader only, fixed to the ground in site-centred east/north metres and continuous across the tile, the 5 m patch and the rings: albedo variation of about ±5 % (7 octaves of value noise, 6.4 m to 0.1 m), micro-relief normals blended onto the LOLA normal as an added height field (so it follows every LOLA slope), and craterlets (radius about 0.1-5 m, below the DEM grid scale) and rock flecks (0.04-0.4 m) drawn in the shading only, with fewer per area as the size grows; since round 6F each craterlet is random in ellipticity (aspect 1.0-1.3) and orientation, rim radius and height (low-frequency noise around the rim, partly broken rims), depth and degradation (most old: shallow, rounded, nearly rimless; a few fresh: sharp rim, steeper wall, slight bright halo), with overlaps, interior albedo variation and lattices rotated and jittered per size class so no rows or columns line up; flecks have irregular outlines and varied size and brightness, and the grain is domain-warped. Sizes and densities are tuned visually, not statistically matched to published crater or rock counts. It multiplies only the Sun term by a bounded factor, so ground facing away from the Sun or in cast shadow is unchanged; its distance is measured from the camera, so it travels with the viewer (round 6F; in round 6E it was in fact measured from the site, because the camera position given to the shader stayed zero for this material type), there is no cut-off near the viewer any more (it was 60-350 m), each octave and feature fades only when it is smaller than a few screen pixels (no shimmer), and the whole texture fades gently between 1.5 and 2 km; no larger features were added for the distance, so far ground keeps exactly the LOLA appearance once the small features are below a pixel; fewer octaves and features on Low and on phones. Faint wheel tracks (two slightly darker strips along the last 250 m or so of the rover's path this session) are drawn the same way. A checkbox (on by default in Walk and Rover, remembered for the session) turns it off. What it is not: no geometry, no vertex displacement and no 3D rocks or boulders (invented boulders could be read as real hazards in a landing-site tool); not a measurement; no effect in Orbit. How it is tested (tests/ui_round6e.js, laptop and phone): with the texture off the picture is pixel-identical to round 6C; Orbit is pixel-identical with it on or off; with shadows on, every pixel the Sun does not reach is unchanged; the mean brightness of the ground band stays within 3 % (round 6E: -1.4 to +2.4 %; round 6F: see ROUND6_REPORT.md); heights, slopes, inspection readouts, rover pose and readout, status tiles, Sun/Earth directions and the shading-consistency score (97.8 / 99.0 / 99.8 %) are unchanged.

16. Mission requirements and Mission casefile: verdicts, not scores (round 6D)

Plain language: You can set the thresholds your mission needs (for example at least 50 % sunlight) and ASTERIA says for each site and date whether each one is Met, Not met or Can't assess, next to the number it judged. It does not add the answers up into a score or pick a winner, because how much each requirement matters depends on the mission. The Mission casefile puts the existing results for one site and time window on one printable page, with where each number comes from and how it was checked.

Technical: Each verdict compares a user threshold with the value printed next to it, at the printed precision (0.1 % / 0.1 h / 0.1°), so a reader can check it by eye; energy uses the Power page's own rule (produced ≥ needed). "Can't assess" is shown with its reason (no 3D terrain tile, so no DEM slope; invalid Power page inputs) instead of a guess. A composite score, a weighting or a confidence percentage was rejected: it would need weights ASTERIA has no basis for, and it would suggest a precision that the inputs (a first-order power estimate, example thresholds, smooth or 240 m horizons for some points) do not have. The casefile aggregates only existing results - the status tiles, window statistics and tables, the Power page model and settings, the Compare slope, the Launch-slip results and the validation result files - so it cannot disagree with the rest of the app and adds no new calculation; tests/ui_round6d.js checks every number in it against the same number elsewhere in the app for a 5 m site, a 20 m site and a custom smooth-horizon point.

Limitations

  • Points outside the map and sites outside the south pole use a smooth Moon horizon - terrain not included. Real hills and crater walls can change when the Sun and Earth rise and set a great deal (compare the terrain and smooth columns on the Validation tab).
  • Points clicked or typed inside the map use terrain from coarser 240 m data: features smaller than about 240 m are smoothed out (see the Validation tab for how much this changes the results).
  • Terrain masks have 1° azimuth resolution and DEM resolution of 20-120 m; boulders and the lander itself are not modelled.
  • "Earth link" uses Earth's centre and a geometric line of sight; antennas, power, ground stations on Earth and relay satellites are not modelled.
  • The Moon is treated as a sphere of radius 1737.4 km for the Sun/Earth directions. Ignoring a site's height shifts the Earth's direction by at most about height ÷ Earth distance (about 0.001° for 6 km) and the Sun's by far less.
