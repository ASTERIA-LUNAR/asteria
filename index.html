This is a 360° panorama of the horizon as seen by a person standing at the site. Left to right is compass direction (0° = toward the lunar north pole, 90° = east). The dotted lines are the paths of the Sun and Earth over the window. Why does the Sun skim the horizon at the pole? The Moon's spin axis is tilted only about 1.5° from perpendicular to its orbit around the Sun, so at the pole the Sun circles around the horizon once a month, rising or sinking by at most about 1.5° over the year. A few metres of hill can decide between sunlight and darkness.

Why does the Earth bob up and down? The Moon always keeps roughly the same face toward Earth, but not exactly: its slightly elliptical, tilted orbit makes Earth appear to wobble by up to about 7° east-west and 6.7° north-south in the lunar sky (this is called libration). Near the limb of the visible face, or at the poles, that wobble moves Earth above and below the horizon, opening and closing direct-to-Earth (DTE) radio windows.

About LunaWindow

LunaWindow is an entry for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 challenge "Lunar Landing Site Navigator". It shows, for any lunar site and date, when the Sun is above the local horizon (solar power) and when the Earth is above it (direct-to-Earth communication), with special attention to the lunar south pole, where the Sun skims the horizon and terrain decides everything.

Not endorsed by NASA. This is an independent hackathon project. It is not a mission planning product and must not be used for operational decisions.

Methods

  • Ephemeris: Sun, Earth and Moon positions from Astronomy Engine (VSOP87 and a Chapront-based lunar theory). Moon orientation from the IAU/WGCCRE rotation model (pole direction and prime-meridian angle). Positions are corrected for light travel time and stellar aberration. Azimuth is measured clockwise from local north (toward the lunar north pole). No atmospheric refraction (the Moon has none).
  • Validation: compared against NASA/JPL Horizons on 52 angle cases and 4 event times; see the Validation tab.
  • Libration is included automatically: the real rotation model and the real Earth-Moon geometry are used, so Earth's wobble in the lunar sky comes out of the computation.
  • Events (sunrise/sunset, Earthrise/Earthset) refer to the centre of the Sun or Earth crossing the horizon, found by a 10-20 minute scan and refined by bisection to 1 minute.
  • Solar disk visible: fraction of the solar disk area above the horizon at the Sun's azimuth, treating the horizon as straight across the 0.53° disk.
  • Terrain horizons (curated south-polar sites only): from LOLA gridded topography (LDEM_85S_20M, 20 m/pixel, and LDEM_80S_80M, 80 m/pixel), ray-marched in 360 directions out to 300 km (or the DEM edge), observer 2 m above the ground, including lunar curvature.

Limitations and approximations

  • Sites without a terrain horizon (all non-polar sites and custom points) use a smooth Moon horizon - terrain not included. Real local hills and crater walls can change rise/set times by hours to days.
  • The Moon is treated as a sphere of radius 1737.4 km; site height above that sphere is taken from LOLA where terrain is used.
  • Terrain masks have 1° azimuth resolution and DEM resolution of 20-80 m; nearby boulders and the lander itself are not modelled.
  • "Earth visible" uses Earth's centre; DTE links also depend on antennas, ground stations on Earth and relay satellites, which are not modelled.
  • Several south-pole entries are representative or feature points, not region centres or landing points; each site card says exactly what the coordinate is.
